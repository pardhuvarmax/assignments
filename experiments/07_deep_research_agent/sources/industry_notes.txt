Internal Memo: General Notes on Four-Day Week Adoption Patterns

Compiled by Strategy & Ops from public reporting and conference talks on
four-day work week adoption, for internal reference (not a formal
literature review).

Two broad models are used elsewhere: (1) full pay for reduced hours, which
is what Acme's pilot used, framed as a productivity/efficiency bet rather
than a cost-cutting move; and (2) a compressed schedule where the same
total hours are worked over four longer days, which preserves output
capacity but delivers less of the wellbeing benefit reported in Acme's
survey data.

Commonly reported failure mode: organizations that adopt the four-day
model without also cutting meeting load or deprioritizing lower-value work
tend to see the "cramming" effect Acme's pilot also flagged -- employees
compress five days of work and stress into four, eroding the wellbeing
benefit within a few months. The organizations that report durable success
generally pair the schedule change with an explicit push to cut recurring
meetings and eliminate low-value work, not just announce a shorter week
and expect the savings to appear on their own.

Commonly reported success factor: client- or customer-facing functions
that adopted a staggered day off (different teams off different days)
report fewer coverage complaints than those that took the whole
organization offline on the same day, consistent with what Client Services
observed in Acme's own pilot.

Industries with the most public four-day week adoption tend to be
knowledge-work and professional services firms with limited
time-of-day-dependent operations (e.g. not manufacturing shift work or
retail). Acme's mix of engineering plus client support with contractual
response-time commitments places it closer to the more complex end of the
adoption spectrum, not the easiest case.
