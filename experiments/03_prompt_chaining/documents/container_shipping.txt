The Container Shipping Revolution

Before the mid-1950s, loading a cargo ship was slow, dangerous, and
expensive. Dockworkers hauled loose crates, barrels, and sacks by hand,
piece by piece, a process called "break bulk" shipping. A single ship
could sit in port for a week or more while it was loaded and unloaded, and
theft of loose cargo was so common that dockside pilferage was treated as
an unavoidable cost of doing business. Freight costs made up such a large
share of the price of traded goods that many manufacturers only sold
regionally, since shipping a product a thousand miles could double or
triple its price by the time it reached a buyer.

The turning point came on April 26, 1956, when a former trucking
entrepreneur named Malcolm McLean loaded 58 custom-built metal containers
onto a converted tanker, the Ideal-X, and sailed it from Newark, New
Jersey to Houston, Texas. Each container could be lifted straight off a
truck chassis and swung onto the ship by crane, then lifted off and placed
directly onto another truck or a railcar at the destination, without
anyone touching the goods inside. McLean's own calculations showed that
loading break-bulk cargo cost around $5.86 per ton, while loading his
containers cost about 16 cents per ton, a roughly 36-fold reduction in
handling cost alone.

Standardization turned McLean's idea into a global system. Through the
1960s, competing container sizes and corner-fitting designs threatened to
fragment the industry, since a shipper's container was useless if a port's
cranes couldn't lift it. The International Organization for Standardization
(ISO) stepped in and, by 1970, had settled on standard container
dimensions, most importantly the 20-foot and 40-foot lengths that are
still dominant today. This gave rise to the "twenty-foot equivalent unit,"
or TEU, the standard measure still used to describe ship and port capacity.
A large modern container ship can carry over 20,000 TEU, compared to the
58 containers McLean's Ideal-X carried on its first voyage.

The economic effects were enormous. Containerization, combined with larger
ships and automated ports, cut ocean freight costs so sharply that
manufacturing shifted toward wherever labor was cheapest, since transport
was no longer the limiting factor. Economists have credited
containerization, alongside trade liberalization, as one of the key
enablers of the late-20th-century boom in globalized manufacturing, since
it became cheap enough to build a component in one country, ship it to
another for assembly, and sell the finished product in a third. Port
cities that adapted early, like Rotterdam and later Singapore and
Shanghai, grew into some of the busiest hubs in the world, while ports
that were slow to build container terminals lost shipping traffic to
rivals.

Modern container shipping is not without fragility. In March 2021, the
container ship Ever Given ran aground diagonally across the Suez Canal,
blocking one of the world's busiest shipping lanes for six days and
delaying an estimated $9 billion of trade per day while it was stuck. The
incident highlighted how concentrated global trade has become around a
small number of canals, ports, and chokepoints: the Suez and Panama
Canals alone carry a large share of east-west maritime trade, and a
blockage or drought (as later affected Panama Canal water levels) can
ripple through supply chains worldwide within days.

Looking ahead, the industry faces pressure to decarbonize, since large
container ships still mostly burn heavy fuel oil and account for a
meaningful share of global shipping emissions. Shipping lines are
experimenting with alternative fuels such as methanol and liquefied
natural gas, and some newly ordered vessels are designed to be
retrofittable as cleaner fuels become commercially viable. At the same
time, ports continue to automate further, with automated cranes and
self-driving yard vehicles reducing turnaround times that, seventy years
ago, were measured in weeks rather than hours.
